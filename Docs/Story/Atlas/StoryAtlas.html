    They came for the stone that holds our island in the sky. We'll make them pay for every pound.

    A railroad baron is mining the lodestone that keeps the frontier islands in the air, and every island his Company strips sinks into the clouds. Rowan Hale came to Skyreach to claim a dead aunt's farm. They end up leading the Rim's homesteaders in a range war for the sky itself.
    Genre and tone Grounded frontier western. Earnest, warm and gritty; the humor comes from people, not jokes.
    Feels like Red Dead Redemption 2, Firefly, the sky of Bioshock Infinite, Shane, Heaven's Gate
    Themes Looking after the land versus stripping it; a community holding each other up; the rich literally rising while the frontier sinks
    Protagonist Rowan Hale, a former railway lineman who inherits the Hale homestead
    Antagonist Augustin Vane, president of the Vane Lodestone & Rail Company
    Hub Skyreach, which grows from a hamlet into the headquarters of the Rim Alliance
    Endgame Range-war defense, train jobs, deep veins, wanted Company officers

    Cold open

    Black screen. An old man's voice, courteous and unhurried: "When I was nine years old, my island fell." A boy clings to a glowing orange stone in an endless white sea of cloud. The light changes from noon to night to noon again. A mail skiff's horn sounds somewhere far off. Cut to nearly sixty years later: the same stone sits under a glass dome on a desk in Concord, and Augustin Vane signs a map of the Rim with a fountain pen. Cut to a mail skiff dropping out of the clouds into Skyreach's farmyard, a passenger asleep on the mail sacks. The pilot kicks their boot. "Wake up, Hale. You're home."

    The world

    • The Reaches are hundreds of islands drifting above the Shroud, a sea of cloud with no known bottom. The old, rich islands near the middle are the Inner Reaches, with the capital, Concord. The outer islands are the Rim, opened to homesteaders eighty years ago by the Homestead Charter: work a claim for five years and it's yours. Skyreach is a Rim island, about as far out as anyone farms.
    • Lodestone keeps the islands up. It's an amber-orange crystal that weighs less than nothing: it falls upward. Every island has a lodestone heart, with roots running through the rock like veins. Cut lodestone lifts airships, holds up cable cars and sets the price of land.
    • Nobody knows who raised the islands. They left traces that folks call Keeper work: stone wells that go down forever, ruined watchtowers, and Reliquaries, granite chests banded in gold with orange rune-lines and a chip of lodestone hovering over the lid.
    • The Vane Lodestone & Rail Company holds the charter to build the Skyline, a railway hung on cables between islands from lodestone pylons, which will tie the Rim to Concord. It also holds the mineral rights under every Rim island, in a clause no homesteader ever read.
    • The secret nobody knows they know. Lodestone is alive, in a slow mineral way, and it grows when it's watered. The Keeper wells carry rainwater down to the heart, and the windmill pumps the old-timers keep turning "because grandpa did" are what keep their islands up. Cut out the heart and sever the roots, and an island sinks a hand's breadth a year, then all at once. The Rim calls the slow sinking the Settling and blames the weather.

    Protagonist: Rowan Hale

    Rowan grew up in Concord's tenements and spent ten years on the Skyline crews: climbing cables, setting charges, laying track over nothing. When a pylon gave way and killed their crew, the Company blamed the dead men for it and docked Rowan's pay for the lost tools. Rowan quit with nothing. A month later a letter came: Aunt Odessa, whom they'd met twice, had died and left them the Hale homestead on Skyreach.

    Rowan arrives meaning to keep their head down, grow potatoes and never think about the Company again. The arc goes from Concord's lesson, the sky doesn't care about you, to the Rim's, we hold each other up: a reluctant neighbor becomes a reluctant leader. Their past is useful. They know how the Company builds, where a pylon is weak and how a train is guarded, and they climb cables and handle explosives like other people tie their boots.

    "I came out here to grow potatoes. Nobody told me the potatoes would need defending."

    Antagonist: Augustin Vane

    Vane is sixty-eight, the president of the Company and the richest man in the Reaches. He was born on Little Calder, a Rim island that sank when he was nine. His parents and sister went down with it. He survived by clinging to a chunk of lodestone in the Shroud for a day and a night until a mail skiff found him. The lesson he took has run his whole life: the sky doesn't care, only stone keeps you up, and whoever owns the stone owns the sky.

    He built the Company from a single salvage barge. He is brilliant, patient, personally kind (he funds orphanages in Concord) and completely certain. He believes the Settling will drop every island in the Reaches within two generations, and his answer is the Firmament: Concord rebuilt on a mountain of harvested lodestone, a city that can never fall. Whoever is inside will live. He calls it a lifeboat: "I can't save everyone. I can save a city."

    What he hides: his own geologist, Dr. Wendell Pell, proved the mining causes the Settling and that lodestone can be regrown. Regrowing takes decades and can't be owned, and the Firmament needs stone now. Vane buried the Pell Report. Pell died in an accident a month later. Vane never asked his Chief Regulator how.

    "Your aunt was a fine woman. She believed the ground loved her back. The ground, Hale, does not love anyone."

    Enemies and creatures

    • The Cinder Gang: raiders paid to burn. Firebugs throw bottles of lamp oil, Torchmen carry homemade flame guns, and Skimmer riders come in low on lodestone sleds.
    • Company Regulators, the main enemy army, carrying the Regulator rifle: riflemen, Breachers with shields and shotguns, Spotters who mark you for mortar crews, Linemen with nail guns and cable hooks, Lifters in lodestone harnesses who float down on you from above, and Ironsides, armored heavies with Thunderdrum shotguns.
    • The Fallen: survivors living on the wreckage of sunk islands. Desperate rather than evil, and hostile to anyone from above.
    • Creatures, driven out of the islands' undersides by the blasting:
      • Meadow Wolf spiders on the Rim's meadows, the first sign something is wrong.
      • MossBacks in the damp, overgrown undersides around the Keeper wells.
      • Cliff Huntsmen lying flat on Hollowmount's cut faces.
      • Ember Tarantulas deep in the mines, where cut lodestone runs hot: dead stone burns.
      • Sky Jumpers nesting on the Skyline's cables and pylons.
      • Stonehogs, boars that root for lodestone pebbles and float a moment when shot in the belly; Gullwyrms, pale cloud serpents that snatch the careless off ledges.

    Skyreach in this story

    The tutorial plays as written, with the story's words:

    1. Move. Rowan climbs out of Gully's skiff in the farmyard with a suitcase and the deed. The house is shut up. Odessa's grave is in the orchard, the dirt still fresh.
    2. Follow the road to the village. The Crossing: a dozen families, a sheriff's office, a blacksmith.
    3. The gun rack. The Crossing keeps a town rifle on the rack in the square "for whoever needs it." Marisol tells Rowan to take it: "Odessa would want her kin armed. Things have been loud lately."
    4. The dummies. The militia's practice range under the windmill. The pump creaks. Marisol: "Keep the wheel turning. Odessa used to say that."
    5. The spiders. Spiders have been boiling up out of the island's underside since the Company started blasting next door. "They're running from something."
    6. The loadout. Rowan checks the town rifle's parts: three other guns' worth, as Abel will say later.
    7. The lookout. The Cinder Gang burned it last spring. From the top, Rowan watches the Company's mining rig on Tallow Flats, the neighboring island, and sees the whole island lurch and drop a dozen meters, its windmill toppling into the clouds. That night the Cinder Gang rides on the Crossing.

    Plot

    Act I: Claim (levels 1–15)

    1. The tutorial, ending with Tallow Flats sinking.
    2. The night raid. The Cinder Gang hits the Crossing. Rowan defends the farmhouse in waves while the barn burns.
    3. Morning. Silas Pruitt arrives with a sympathetic face and an offer for the Hale claim. After he leaves, Marisol tells Rowan that Odessa didn't fall from the plateau by accident: she had turned Pruitt down three times.
    4. Under the farmhouse floor, Rowan finds Odessa's Settling Book: thirty years of measured island heights. Every island near a Company dig sank faster than the rest. Tucked inside is her lodestone charm.
    5. Tallow Flats. Rowan helps Granny June hold her homestead, fights the Cinder Gang through the tilted wheat and corners Matches Molloy in the burning silo. Before he dies he laughs: the Company pays by the acre burned.
    6. Theo Lark deserts from the Company's survey team and brings the dig map. Skyreach is next.
    7. Marisol, Granny June, Theo, Gully and Rowan found the Rim Alliance. Gully's skiff route becomes its network. The Company declares the Alliance outlaws, and the Regulators come.

    Act II: The Line (levels 15–38)

    1. The Skyline. The Alliance robs Company trains for rifles, payroll and paperwork: train jobs across trestles over the clouds. Rowan knows these trains; they helped build the line. The Starling twins fight them on the roof of a moving train.
    2. Hollowmount. The miners are paid in Company scrip and kept in debt. Big Ida Stroud fights Rowan from the Bore Mule while the floor gives way. If Rowan saves her trapped miners during the fight instead of chasing the kill, Ida later brings her crews over to the Alliance.
    3. Tumbledown. Odessa's book points to the islands that sank a decade ago. Rowan goes down to the Shelf and finds Mayor Kell and the Fallen, who want everyone above to know what falling is like. Kell grew up with Vane on Little Calder and survived the same fall. Two survivors of one fall: one decided to own the stone, the other to drag everyone down.
    4. Gallows Reach. Marisol is captured in a raid. Rowan breaks her out of the Company jail, cracks the records office and steals the Pell Report. Chief Regulator Crowe is waiting. After the fight, his ledger has an entry in his neat hand: O. Hale, the plateau, forty crowns.
    5. The turn. Theo decodes the report: lodestone grows when watered, the Keeper wells feed it, and the mining causes the Settling. Keep the wheel turning. Odessa knew, or guessed.
    6. The fall of Skyreach. Vane strikes back. Ironclads shell the Crossing, sappers blow Skyreach's Keeper well and the windmill comes down. The island drops thirty meters in one night. The waterfall stops and the pond drains. It's the lowest point of the story, and the hub shows it.

    Act III: Keep the Wheel Turning (levels 38–50)

    1. The Taproot. Rowan goes beneath the biggest Rim island to the Keeper heart. The halls show how the wells were built to water the stone. The Well-Wyrm, a blind white serpent that has lived in the wells for centuries, guards the way.
    2. Raising Skyreach. The Alliance rebuilds the well and the windmill: Ida's miners, the Tallows, even some of the Fallen. The wheel turns, water runs down into the island, and Skyreach rises a hand's breadth. The waterfall trickles back. It's the first hope in a long time.
    3. Hesper. Rowan sends the Pell Report to Hesper Vane under a flag of truce. She reads it, confronts her father, and brings the Steadfast over to the Rim.
    4. Concord. The Alliance hits the capital while the Firmament's foundation rises on harvested stone, and Hesper's Steadfast duels her father's flagship, the Providence, over the docks. Vane isn't aboard. Company surveyors trailed Rowan into the Taproot, so he now knows exactly where the Rim's heart is. The Deep Bore has already left to drill it, and drilling it will drop thirty Rim islands at once.
    5. The finale. The Alliance fleet and the Steadfast against the Company over the Taproot, then down into the halls. The Deep Bore is a colossal drilling engine with Vane at the controls, fought in phases as it grinds toward the heart.

    Ending

    The Bore breaks apart and its rig tears loose over the Shroud with Vane on it, clinging to a chunk of lodestone exactly as he did at nine. Rowan can reach for his hand or let go.

    • Reach, and Vane lives to stand trial. He testifies, the Charter's mineral clause is struck down and the Company is broken up.
    • Let go, and he falls with his machine, still holding the stone.

    Either way, the Firmament stops rising and the harvested stone goes back. Epilogue: Skyreach in the afternoon. The windmill turns, the waterfall runs, Gully's skiff lands with the mail. Rowan opens the Settling Book and writes this year's height, a few meters higher than last year's.

    Side quests (samples)

    • The Widow's Well. An old woman on a tiny island has kept her Keeper pump turning by hand for forty years. Her island is the only one near a dig that hasn't sunk. She thinks it's luck.
    • Scrip. Hollowmount miners want their debts burned. Rob the Company store's ledger safe (a Strongbox) and decide whether to burn the books or keep them as evidence.
    • The Mail Must Go Through. Gully is ambushed by Skimmer riders. Escort the skiff through a Gullwyrm nest.
    • Little Calder. Find the wreck of Vane's childhood island on the Tumbledown shelf. In the ruins of his family's house is a child's drawing of an island with a windmill on it.

    Loot in this world

    • Who makes the guns. Homestead Ironworks makes farm guns in walnut and iron. Regulator Arms makes the Company's precise blued rifles. Kestrel Aeronautic makes light bullpups for airship crews, Scrapjack rebuilds wrecks, and Thunderdrum Ordnance makes the Company's heavy shotguns. Rim smiths like Abel take all of them apart and put them back together, which is the parts system.
    • Rarity and the beams. Every gun carries a sliver of lodestone in its action to lighten it and steady the recoil. When a gun leaves its dead owner's hand, its sliver flares, which is the loot beam: grey slag, then green, blue, purple, and orange heartstone.
    • Chests. Supply Crates are Company supply drops along the Skyline. Strongboxes are Company payroll safes, the prize of every train job. Reliquaries are Keeper caches in old ruins, each holding a weapon set with heartstone.
    • Elements (when they come). Fire from the Cinder Gang, Shock from the Company's galvanic rounds, Corrosive from spider venom and mine acid, and Lift, the setting's own: heartstone rounds make a target weightless, so it drifts up and, near an edge, away.

    Endgame (levels 50–70)

    • Range War. Company remnants and Cinder holdouts raid Alliance islands. Defend the windmills in waves, at rising threat tiers.
    • Train Jobs. Repeatable heists on armored Company treasure trains with Strongbox payrolls, with modifiers such as armored cars, an ironclad escort or a burning trestle.
    • Deep Veins. Mine descents under the islands that get hotter with depth (Ember Tarantulas, dead stone). The best heartstone is at the bottom.
    • Wanted. Bounty posters for Company officers who fled with stone: named elites with rotating legendary drops.
    • Regrowth. Restore Keeper wells on your claim. Each one raises its island a little and unlocks a reward.

    Production notes

    • Reuses: the tutorial island as the hub (burned, sunk and rebuilt states are variants of one level); the windmill becomes the story's symbol; the three chests fit as they are; the Regulator, Kestrel, Homestead, Scrapjack and Thunderdrum designs become gunsmiths.
    • New: a train level (cars moving along a fixed track while the world scrolls past); hub states as swappable sublevels; wave-defense mode; ironclads as set pieces you fight on and around, never vehicles you steer.
    • Risk: the lowest of the eight. Everything stays in the frontier art kit already built.

    Why pick it, and what to watch

    Pick it for the closest fit to what's built, a heartfelt story about people, and a hub that visibly grows and suffers with the story. Watch out: "a western in the sky" has been done (Bioshock Infinite, Skies of Arcadia), and "sacrifice the few to save the city" is a familiar villain. Its strength has to come from Vane's history, the lodestone secret and the sinking islands, so lean on those.

    Dusk on the lookout. Seven riders, a smashed Reliquary and a burning orange ember in a gloved hand. Ellis Ransom, youngest of the gang, steps between the Deacon and the stairs: "Not here. Not my home." The Deacon's face, sad and fond. "I'm sorry, kid." One shot, and the sky turns over.

    A week later: birdsong, a bright blue afternoon, bees in the orchard. A hand breaks through the dirt of a fresh grave.

    The world

    • In the Reaches the dead are given to the wind. Farm families bury their dead for seven days to say goodbye, then lay them on a burial board at the island's edge, and at dusk the Gravewind, a cold wind that blows only then, carries their souls past the edge of the sky to the Far Shore.
    • Every island has a saint: its first settler, laid in a granite Reliquary banded in gold, whose soul stays behind as an ember of orange light floating over the lid. The saints are lighthouses. Their light shows the dead the way to the Far Shore. Nobody touches a Reliquary, ever.
    • An island that loses its saint loses its way: its dead stop crossing. They linger as the Unpaid, cold, hungry and angry, and the living there sicken and go strange.
    • Six months ago the Dunne Gang, eight outlaws led by a defrocked preacher, was hired by a stranger who paid in old gold to rob seven Reliquaries on seven islands and take the embers. Seven of them ended up carrying one apiece (Ellis never would; something about the embers felt wrong), and each ember changed its carrier. Six of them became the Hollow: a saint's power soured inside a living sinner. (The Deacon fought his ember, and it's killing him instead.) No living hand can kill a Hollow. It takes someone already dead.
    • The seventh job was Skyreach, Ellis's home. Ellis balked, and the Deacon shot them.

    Protagonist: Ellis Ransom

    Ellis grew up on the Ransom farm on Skyreach and left at sixteen to ride with the Deacon, a charismatic outlaw-preacher who took in strays and called them family. Ten years of stagecoach jobs and bank jobs later, Ellis was the youngest and the best shot in the gang, and the one the Deacon loved most.

    Now Ellis is a Revenant: cold to the touch, no heartbeat, wounds that close, and eyes that see souls. Ellis wants three things, in the order they admit them: revenge, rest, and to make right what they helped do to Skyreach's dead. The arc runs from vengeance to atonement. The story's real question isn't whether Ellis can kill seven Hollow. It's whether Ellis can forgive the one who shot them, and themself.

    "I'm not here to kill you, Ned. You're already dead. I'm here to collect."

    Antagonist: Mister Sexton

    He is tall and patient and always polite, in an undertaker's coat and a stovepipe hat, his face in shadow even at noon. He smells like rain and keeps a ledger. He sits on the lookout railing, legs crossed, and offers Ellis a deal: bring back the seven embers and their soul will be released to rest. Fail, and Ellis rides the Gravewind forever with no shore at the end.

    What he is: before there were saints, the dead needed a ferryman to cross, and every soul paid him. When the first settlers became saints and lit the way, the dead stopped needing him. He has been starving at the edge of the sky ever since. He hired the gang. Now he needs a Revenant to pry the embers back out of the Hollow so he can snuff them at the Toll Gate. With every saint dark, every soul in the Reaches would have to pay his toll again, and the ones who can't pay would serve him.

    "Everyone pays the ferryman, friend. I'm only asking for what's owed."

    The Deacon, Ambrose Dunne

    The gang's leader and Ellis's mentor, almost a father. A preacher thrown out of his church for saying heaven was a toll road, he kept Skyreach's ember for himself. Unlike the others he didn't go Hollow. He fought it, and it's killing him instead. He shot Ellis because Ellis drew on him first and he was faster. There was no secret mercy in it, and he has been sick about it every day since. He's the last name on the list and the hardest.

    "I've buried a lot of people, kid. You're the only one who ever came back to ask me why."

    Plot

    Act I: Dust to Dust (levels 1–16)

    1. The cold open, the grave, the tutorial and the deal on the lookout. Sexton's terms: bring each ember to the lookout at the new moon. Each one paid returns a little of Ellis's life: warmth, color, a heartbeat for a minute.
    2. The Gilded Lily. Ned Purcell runs a floating casino where the house always wins. The house loses. His ember goes to Sexton, and Ellis feels the sun on their skin for the first time since dying.
    3. Dustwater Mesa. Ira Gale, inside his storm. Dying, he coughs: "He paid us in old gold, Ellis. Gold that'd been under the clouds."
    4. Ranger Ruth Calder ambushes Ellis outside Dustwater: dead or not, Ellis rode with the Dunne Gang. Then the Unpaid pour out of the dust and the two of them fight back to back. It's an uneasy truce.

    Act II: The Unpaid (levels 16–40)

    1. Saint Agnes Mire. Sister Constance raises the dead because she thinks she's helping them, since they can't cross. Before the end she tells Ellis: "The saints don't hold souls back, child. They light the way. Put them out and the dead can't find the shore. And somebody's waiting at the gate to sell them directions."
    2. Furnace Hollow. Barrels Kessler, burning from the inside out. His foundry is full of the Unpaid of the island he robbed.
    3. The Needles. Lena Okoro describes the man who hired them: tall, polite, a stovepipe hat.
    4. Hob confesses. He was a Revenant once, eighty years ago. He paid his debt in full, and Sexton made him a crow. "Every Revenant he makes ends up with feathers, kid. Ask me how I know."
    5. Stoneweight Quarry. Tobias Grant doesn't want to fight; he offers his ember if Ellis lets his crew walk. Ellis can accept, but Sexton's Mourners arrive and force the fight anyway.
    6. The new moon. Ellis comes to the lookout with the sixth ember and refuses to hand it over. Sexton stops being polite. He takes it, and Ellis dies again and wakes in the orchard grave, greyer and colder than before. Sexton now holds six saints. He needs one more: Skyreach's, in the Deacon's pocket.

    Act III: The Gravewind (levels 40–50)

    1. Ellis, Ruth, Tilly and Hob make a plan: reach the Deacon before Sexton does, keep Skyreach's ember, take the other six back at the Toll Gate.
    2. Gravesend. The necropolis, crawling with the Unpaid. The Deacon is dying in a mausoleum, holding Skyreach's ember to his chest. Ellis and the Deacon finally talk: no excuses, no secret reasons, just a coward's confession and a kid who came home. The Mourners attack, and for one fight the Deacon stands beside Ellis again.
    3. The Hollow Deacon. He dies, and Sexton climbs into his body. Ellis has to fight their mentor's corpse while his own voice keeps apologizing. When it's over, Skyreach's ember is in Ellis's hand.
    4. The Toll Gate. At dusk Ellis steps off the lookout and rides the Gravewind to the edge of the sky: a pier of grey wood, a lantern, and every unpaid soul in the Reaches standing in line. Sexton at the gate is a towering ferryman made of black cloth and crows, with a long rifle called Toll. Hob flies at his face.

    Ending

    The six embers spill free and Ellis carries them, and Skyreach's, home: seven saints relit, seven islands whose dead can finally cross. The Gravewind blows, and thousands of souls walk past Ellis to the Far Shore. The Deacon is among them, and he tips his hat. Ellis's debt is paid. They could go too.

    They don't. Sexton's ledger, now in Ellis's hand, is full of souls who slipped away while the saints were dark. "Not yet." Hob settles on their shoulder. Grandma Delia sets a plate.

    Loot in this world

    • Who makes the guns. Frontier makers: Coachman and Breacher shotguns, Regulator rifles, Homestead farm guns, Kestrel carbines. Out here, guns come off the dead and get rebuilt by whoever's handy, so every gun is a patchwork of other guns' parts. The Hollow's guns are soul-forged irons: weapons that killed so much that the dead stayed in them.

    Production notes

    • Reuses: the western art kit as it is; graves, crosses and burial boards are cheap props; the Reliquary is the story's centerpiece.
    • New: ghost enemies (translucent, phasing: emissive and dithered, priced on Medium before anything else); a Grave Sight vision mode (a post-process pass, measured); night lighting for some areas; eight bespoke boss fights (the seven and Sexton), the main cost.
    • Risk: medium. The boss roster is big, but each boss anchors one area and one set of enemies, so the work comes in clean pieces.

    Why pick it, and what to watch

    Pick it for the crispest structure of the eight: seven names on a list from the first hour, each one a place, a boss and a legendary. It's a western that fits the art already built, with a supernatural layer that triples the enemy variety without a second art kit, and an endgame (the Ledger) the story hands you directly. Watch out: it's darker than the sunny meadow suggests (that contrast is the hook, so own it). Eight bosses need eight distinct fights. And "the patron is the villain" has to be seeded fairly, through Hob, Constance and Lena, so the turn feels earned.

    The world

    • The open sky. Free islands drift on the trade winds in slow circuits, and anything that floats can be sailed: airships ride lodestone ballast and canvas. Where there's trade there are pirates.
    • The Meridian Admiralty rules the wealthy inner islands and claims the whole sky, but can't hold the outer drift. Its dream is the Meridian: every island chained into a fixed lattice under navy guns.
    • The Calico Queen. Thirty years ago Captain Isadora Drake ruled the outer sky with a fleet of black-and-gold ships. When the Admiralty finally cornered her, her flagship flew into a cloud bank and was never seen again, and with it went the Calico Hoard: the plunder of a hundred ships and something called the Heart of the Sky. The map to it was split in two and given to the two people she trusted most: her quartermaster, and the cabin girl she was raising as her own.
    • The secret. The Calico Queen's flagship was never an ordinary ship. It was the Lantern, a vessel the size of an island, built by the vanished Keepers who raised the sky, with a Helm that can steer any island, not just itself. After her last battle, Isadora hid it in plain sight: she let it drift to the edge of the sky, let soil and grass grow over its decks, built a farm on top, and retired. The Lantern is Skyreach. The windmill is its mast, the ruined lookout is its helm tower, and Granny Nell Thistlewood is the Calico Queen.

    Protagonist: Tam Reed

    Found as a baby in a lifeboat drifting past Skyreach and raised by Granny Nell on the Thistlewood farm, Tam is a quick, curious, chore-dodging twenty-year-old who has spent every evening of their life on the ruined lookout, watching airships pass and wishing. Tam can shoot, because Granny insisted, and has no idea why Granny shoots like that.

    The arc runs from wanting to leave home to understanding what home was, and from deckhand to first mate to captain. Tam learns that people are more than the worst thing they did, and less than the legends about them.

    "I've wanted to see the world my whole life. Nobody mentioned the world shoots back."

    Antagonist: Grand Admiral Severin Holt

    Cold, precise and incorruptible, Holt commands the Meridian Admiralty's fleet from the dreadnought Inexorable. When he was nine, the Red Wake pirates burned his home island while he hid in a flour barrel. Everything since has been in service of one idea: a sky where nothing drifts without a flag and no child is ever raided again. He wants the Lantern's Helm to chain every free island into the Meridian at once. He isn't cruel. He just doesn't believe in freedom, because he's seen what it costs.

    "Order is a mercy, child. The sky has simply never been shown any."

    Captain Margo Sable

    The other face of the antagonist. Margo is the captain of the Sweet Persistence: charming, scarred, funny, generous, a brilliant sailor and a better liar. She rescues Tam, teaches them the sky and becomes the mentor they always wanted. She holds the other half of the map, and she'll sell anyone, Tam included, to finally get what she's owed. Then she'll regret it.

    1. The tutorial, ending with the crash. In the orchard, a dying old pirate presses an etched brass half-map, a battered compass and a clockwork parrot into Tam's hands: "Find the Queen. Tell her Margo's coming. Tell her the Lantern's waking."
    2. Crale's Gallows Bird lands raiders to take the map. Tam and Granny defend the farmhouse, and Granny shoots like nobody Tam has ever seen.
    3. The Sweet Persistence swoops out of the clouds and blasts the Gallows Bird. Captain Margo Sable charms everyone, admires the map, and offers Tam a berth: "That map's worth a fortune, and you're worth more alive." Granny goes very still when she sees Margo's face, says nothing, and lets Tam go: "Keep your powder dry, and your captain closer." Admiral Feathers squawks "Captain!" as Granny speaks. Tam assumes he means Margo.
    4. Port Gallant. The legend of the Calico Queen in every tavern; Bash signs on. The half-map's marks point to the Queen's old haunts. A brawl with the Red Wake ends with Cleaver Moll.
    5. The Shoals. Diving the wrecks of the Calico decoy fleet. A logbook says the Admiralty took a brass key off the Queen's ship, the Calico Key, and keeps it as a trophy at Gallowglass. Old Knotty has made the decoy fleet her nest.
    6. Crale ambushes the Sweet Persistence in a cloud bank. A boarding fight across two decks; Crale escapes.

    Act II: The Tempest (levels 15–40)

    1. Gallowglass Fort. Infiltrate the Admiralty fortress, quietly or not, and steal the Calico Key from the trophy hall. Commander Varga duels Tam on the battlements. Beaten, she lets them go. She has her own opinion of Holt.
    2. The Brass Cemetery. The Calico crew's graves, the scavenger clans and Crale's last stand aboard a crashed galleon. Dying, he sneers: "Ask your captain who she was before. Ask her about the Queen's cabin girl."
    3. Margo tells Tam the truth, or most of it: the cabin girl, the capture, eleven years on a prison barge, the Queen who never came back for her. She shows Tam the other half of the map. Together the halves chart a course through the Tempest Wall, then trail off into a blank: the last mark only shows under the Calico Key's lens. Tam trusts her.
    4. The Tempest Wall. Storm islands, lightning, the eye of the storm, and the Admiralty fleet waiting in it. Margo has traded Tam, the map and the Key for a full pardon and a share. The crew is captured. Holt is courteous about it.
    5. The Penance. A prison break. Admiral Feathers steals the cell keys. On the way out, in Holt's cabin, the joined map lies under the Calico Key's lens, and the X is Skyreach. Feathers takes the Key too, and the crew escapes on a stolen launch. Doc Pembrook goes pale: thirty years ago he watched the Queen's flagship fly into a cloud and come out an island. Then he hums the Calico Queen's battle shanty, the one Granny hums over her peas, and Tam knows.

    Act III: The Lantern (levels 40–50)

    1. Skyreach besieged. Holt's fleet got there first. Marines are camped in the Crossing. Then the villagers pull cutlasses and blunderbusses out from under their floorboards: the sheriff, the blacksmith, the barkeep, the whole retired Calico crew. Granny Nell walks out of the farmhouse in a black-and-gold coat: "Name's Isadora Drake. Get off my farm."
    2. The Hold. Tam and Isadora go down through the farmhouse cellar into the Lantern's ancient hull, past Keeper guardians waking from thirty years' sleep, to the Helm. The Calico Key turns.
    3. The Lantern wakes. The windmill's tower rises and its blades unfold into great sails. The ruined lookout lifts into a helm tower. The island tears loose from its drift and sails into the Admiralty fleet.
    4. Aboard the Inexorable, Margo watches the island come, and spikes the dreadnought's main guns before they can fire.
    5. The Inexorable. The crew boards the dreadnought and fights deck by deck to the bridge, then down to the stolen Keeper engine Holt means to ram the Lantern with. Holt fights like he commands: patient, exact, surrounded by his best.

    Ending

    The Inexorable breaks and falls. Tam can pull Holt from the wreck to stand trial, or leave him to his ship. With the Helm, Tam does what Isadora never dared: one turn of the wheel and the Meridian's chains snap, and every island the Admiralty held drifts free. Then Tam locks the Helm's hold on other islands for good.

    On the deck, Isadora and Margo face each other as themselves for the first time in thirty years. "For thirty years I thought the Admiralty hanged you, Margo. If I'd known, I'd have torn the sky apart." Margo laughs, cries a little, and takes exactly one small chest from the Hoard before sailing off with a salute. "She always did take the small chest," Isadora says. Then she hands Tam the wheel. "She's yours now. Try not to sink her."

    Production notes

    • Reuses: the tutorial island becomes the final act's battlefield and the endgame's hub: the biggest payoff any story gives the existing level. The windmill becomes the mast and the lookout the helm.
    • New: airship decks as small levels (the Sweet Persistence's deck is the hub for Acts I and II); scripted boarding sequences; the island-sails sequence (new windmill parts, wind, a sky dome and clouds that scroll so the island seems to move while the level stays put); storm effects, priced on Medium.
    • Keep ships as set pieces you fight on, not vehicles you steer. Steering is a different game and a big bill.
    • Risk: medium to high, mostly from airship content.

    Why pick it, and what to watch

    Pick it for the most fun and the widest appeal of the eight, a crew that becomes a family, and the best payoff for the island already built: Skyreach turns out to be the treasure and then becomes your ship. Watch out: airship content is expensive, so keep the ship scenes as set pieces. The lighter tone must stay grimy (pirates are dirty), so it matches "rustic, harsh, lived-in." And the Treasure Island structure is familiar, so the Granny reveal has to land. Seed it from the first scene.

    Cold open

    Three years ago, on the bow of a harpoon frigate. A titan called Marrowdeep fills the sky, an eye as big as a lake slowly turning toward the ship. A young harpooner raises the Lance. A woman's voice, calm: "Now, Sloane." Then, from somewhere in the moss on the titan's back, a small high cry: a calf. The harpooner lowers the Lance. Everything goes wrong at once: ropes snap, men fall, the frigate lists, and the titan screams as other harpoons hit home.

    Today: Sloane wakes in a farm bunkhouse on Skyreach, soaked in sweat. Through the window: wheat, a windmill, a perfect afternoon. A cup of water on the sill trembles, all by itself.

    The world

    • The titans. Beings the size of islands drift on the high winds, sleeping for centuries at a time. Soil, rivers, forests and towns gather on their backs. Most people have no idea their island is alive. Old folks know it "breathes" once a year, rising and settling a meter on the night of the Long Breath festival.
    • Amber. A titan's heart is a mass of glowing orange crystal that makes it lighter than air. Amber also lights lamps, lifts airships and goes into the finest guns.
    • Stillbacks. Dead titans turn to stone, their amber dims, and they slowly sink. The great cities are built on stillbacks, and the cities are sinking with them.
    • The Listeners were the old priests who could hear titan-song and guide the beasts. Their order is all but gone; their monasteries are ruins.
    • The Spear began as whalers, harvesting amber from dead titans. Forty-five years ago, a titan named Bellwether woke beneath the city of Harrowgate and rolled over. Ten thousand people fell. Since Ysolde took command, the Spear has been a crusade: kill every titan before it wakes, even if people live on its back. A quick fall is a mercy next to a waking.
    • The secret. The titans are waking because the Migration has come. Once every few centuries they travel to the Calving Grounds at the edge of the sky to give birth and to die. A titan that wakes gently keeps its back level and carries its people with it. A frightened titan rolls. And nothing frightens titans like the death-scream of another titan's amber. The Spear is causing the disaster it fights.

    Protagonist: Sloane Teague

    Sloane was the best harpooner of their generation, Ysolde Harrowgate's pick, with eleven titan kills before they were twenty-five. During the hunt of Marrowdeep they heard what no harpooner should, a calf crying, and froze. The hunt went wrong, crewmates died, and Marrowdeep died anyway. Sloane deserted that night. For three years they've worked the Abernathy farm on Skyreach under a false name, trying to forget the song they keep hearing in their sleep.

    Sloane is a latent Listener. The arc runs from hiding to guarding, from killer to Listener, from guilt to the harder thing, which is doing better. They start the game already deadly: the tutorial is an old soldier finding out their hands still remember.

    "I've killed eleven titans. I can still hear every one of them."

    Antagonist: Captain-General Ysolde Harrowgate

    Ysolde was nine when Bellwether woke under Harrowgate. Her father threw her aboard the last airship leaving as the city tipped into the sky. She took the city's name, joined the whalers, and turned them into the Spear. Now in her fifties, grey and iron-straight, with one hand replaced by a harpoon-launcher, she lights ten thousand candles every year on the anniversary. Her crews would die for her.

    She doesn't hate titans. She sees them as floods with a heartbeat, natural disasters waiting to happen to everyone living on their backs, and she has watched a waking. Her methods are monstrous: she kills titans with towns still on them, after an evacuation if there's time. Her logic is hard to argue with, until Sloane learns the Spear is making the wakings worse. Her final goal is to kill the Sovereign, the eldest titan, the size of a country, with the Leviathan Lance, a cannon that drives a spike of hardened amber into a titan's heart. Kill the Sovereign and the Migration ends forever.

    Sloane was her protégé. She still calls Sloane her best lance, and she'd still take them back.

    "I don't hate them, Sloane. I just love us more."

    Her lieutenants (bosses)

    • Flensemaster Corwin Mott runs the Driftmoor flensing yards, where titans are cut up for amber. A butcher who loves his saws.
    • Bosun Greta Brannock commands the harpoon frigate Penitent. Practical, loyal, not cruel.
    • Lamplighter Ines Vex is the Spear's amber alchemist. She has learned to make dead titans move again with amber injections, as weapons: the Stitched.
    • Lancemaster Odran Hask guards the Leviathan Lance's foundry at Harrowgate Spire.

    Plot

    Act I: The Long Breath (levels 1–15)

    1. The tutorial, ending with Old Hollow's breath. Sloane names the calf Biscuit, mostly to annoy Dr. Quist later.
    2. The Spear lands on Skyreach the next morning to "evacuate" the village before they kill the island. Their captain recognizes Sloane. Sloane digs up their old lance-rifle, and the farm fights. The Spear pulls back to wait for orders.
    3. Maren Ashdown tells Sloane what Old Hollow is, and what the breath means: "She isn't waking angry, child. She's waking to leave. The Migration has come." To guide a waking, Sloane needs the Listeners' Songbook, last kept in a monastery on Coldback. The way there starts at Driftmoor.
    4. Driftmoor. Sneak through the whaling port inside a dead titan's skull. Rescue Dr. Quist from Mott's flensing yard, where she was caught studying a "specimen." She takes one look at Biscuit and joins for good.
    5. The Grazer Herd. The Penitent is hunting a grazing herd. Sloane runs across the moving backs, cuts harpoon lines and fights Brannock on her own deck. As the herd escapes, it sings, and for the first time Sloane hears it clearly, not as a nightmare but as music.
    6. A letter arrives by glider, in Ysolde's square hand: "Come home, Sloane. I'll forgive the desertion. I need my best lance for the Sovereign." Sloane burns it.

    Act II: The Song (levels 15–36)

    1. Thornback. An uneasy deal with Kip Ortolan's poachers to cross the desert titan. A Mawlamprey has burrowed in near its heart; killing it lets the titan sing again. Kip sells Sloane out once, apologizes twice, and turns up later anyway.
    2. Coldback. The Listener monastery in the ice, the Rimewyrm in the caves below, and the Songbook. Sloane learns to use an amber resonator, a singing stone that calms or steers creatures. Maren's lessons begin to work. The Songbook also says what the Listeners feared: a titan's death-scream terrifies every titan that hears it. The Spear makes the violent wakings.
    3. The Gullet. Inside a dead titan, Ines Vex's laboratory and her proudest work: a Stitched titan, fought from the inside as it moves. Her notes reveal the plan. The Leviathan Lance is finished, and its target is the Sovereign, the eldest, whose song leads the whole Migration. If it dies screaming, a thousand titans wake at once in terror.
    4. Old Hollow wakes. The Spear strikes Skyreach as it wakes for good. Sloane stands on the lookout, the titan's brow, singing it calm through the resonator while Spear boarders come over the cliffs. Old Hollow rises, slowly, and keeps its back level. Nobody falls. The farm, the village and the windmill all ride a living titan into the sky and join the Migration. From here on, the hub travels.

    Act III: The Migration (levels 36–50)

    1. Harrowgate Spire. Storm the Spear's fortress to stop the Lance, and arrive a day late: Ysolde's flagship, the Vigil, has already sailed with it. Free the captive Listeners in the cells, beat Lancemaster Hask, and take the Lance's firing schematics.
    2. The Sovereign. It rises out of a cloud bank like a continent, with whole ruined cities on its back and the Migration's herd gathered around it. Ysolde's fleet attacks. The final battle crosses the Sovereign's back as it wakes, Old Hollow's villagers firing from their own titan alongside it.
    3. Ysolde. On the Lance platform, in her harpoon rig, while the Lance charges. She fights like Sloane's teacher, because she was. Stop the charge, survive her, and the Sovereign sings.

    Ending

    Ysolde hangs from the wrecked Lance platform as the Sovereign rises. Sloane can pull her up. If saved, she watches the Migration pass in silence, then lights a single candle. Or Sloane can let the Spear's commander fall the way Harrowgate fell.

    The Migration reaches the Calving Grounds. In the last scene, the farmers of Skyreach watch from the lookout as Old Hollow gives birth to a calf the size of a hill. Biscuit floats out to meet its kind, turns back once, and stays with Sloane.

    • Moving islands are an illusion. The titan you stand on is a static level. Motion comes from the sky dome and the distant titans moving, scrolling clouds, wind, and a slow camera sway. A "breath" is a camera shake and a sky shift. Never move the level's collision.
    • Creature-first: the rig pipeline that made the spider (Blender rig, code-driven motion) carries the whole cast. Distant titans are huge low-detail silhouettes, cheap to draw.
    • Colossus fights (climbing a moving creature) are the most expensive feature here. Make only a few, and set the first one inside the Gullet, where the "moving" titan is an interior.
    • New: harpoon and grapple traversal; the resonator (calm or redirect creatures); interior body levels.
    • Risk: high, and the most original premise of the eight.

    Why pick it, and what to watch

    Pick it for the most original premise here, a creature-first world that gives the bestiary a reason to exist, huge marketable images, and a villain with a real argument. Watch out: giant moving creatures are expensive, so keep the illusion cheap and the colossus fights few. A gentle titan-hugging story can lose the shooter, but the Spear, the poachers and the Stitched keep the guns busy.

    Cold open

    White text types itself onto black, like a terminal: HERITAGE HABITAT 114 — SKYREACH. POPULATION 61. WARDEN ASSIGNED: NONE. A pause. UNIT W-7: DECOMMISSIONED. Cut to a body falling past clouds, hitting a pond and drifting down a creek toward the edge of the world. Joel Bellamy, fishing, drops his rod and hauls them out a few meters short of the falls. "Hattie! HATTIE! There's a person in the creek!"

    The world

    • Four hundred years ago, the surface was buried by the Grey, a self-replicating dust that choked everything below a certain height. The Halvorsen-Adair Lift Consortium built the floating islands as refuges, each held up by a lift engine around a glowing orange lattice-core.
    • The Sleepers. The Consortium's founders and investors, forty thousand of the richest people alive, went into cryogenic vaults to wait out the Grey: we'll wake when the world is clean.
    • The Heritage Program. To keep the islands running, the Consortium settled them with caretakers, workers and their families, in Heritage Habitats: deliberately simple frontier lives, so the caretakers would be self-sufficient, wouldn't tamper with the machines and wouldn't need much. Their descendants are today's frontier folk. They know nothing. Their laws, their almanacs and the Charter read in church every Sunday were written by the Consortium.
    • The Wardens are synthetic guardians built to look and live human. They maintain the engines, keep the peace and protect the vaults. Most were shut down over the centuries. A few never were, and the best known is the Marshal, the legendary lawman of the Reaches. Folk think "the Marshal" is a title passed down. It's the same man, four hundred years old.
    • The secret. The Grey burned itself out a hundred and fifty years ago. The surface is green again. The Consortium's systems saw it and started the Dawn Protocol to wake the Sleepers, and the Marshal has been holding it back ever since. Now it has started on its own.
    • What folks call Keeper relics are Consortium hardware: Reliquaries are supply vaults, their orange "runes" are interface text, and the crystal floating over the lid is a lattice-core.

    Protagonist: Ward

    The Bellamys named them Ward, as in a ward of the family. Hattie thought it was sweet. Ward has worked the farm for three years: eats, sleeps, bleeds, burns the biscuits, and is good with tools in a way nobody can explain. Clem, the Bellamys' twelve-year-old, thinks Ward is the most interesting person alive.

    Ward is W-7, a Warden. Three years ago they found out the truth, that the Sleepers were mostly dead and the surface was green, and wanted to tell the folk. The Marshal shot them and threw them off the Anchor. Ward fell a very long way and landed in Skyreach's pond, their memory gone.

    The arc: am I a person? becomes I chose these people, and that was real. Ward was built to serve the Sleepers and chooses the farmers. Gameplay grows with the identity: each restored memory is a new ability.

    "I bleed, I sleep, I burn the biscuits. If that's a machine, it's a very convincing one."

    Antagonist: the Marshal

    Marshal Josiah Gentry, Warden Prime: duster, silver star, a grey mustache, kind eyes. He has kept the peace in the Reaches for four hundred years. He remembers every person he has buried, and he has buried generations. Folk tell stories about him the way people tell stories about saints. He is good at his job, and he loves the frontier.

    He believes in the Charter: the islands belong to the Sleepers, and the folk are their caretakers. He held back the Dawn Protocol for a hundred and fifty years because he was afraid of what the Sleepers would do to "his people." Now his own core is failing and the Protocol has started without him. So he'll hand the islands over in good order, and that means quietly removing every settlement that has learned too much, so the Sleepers wake to find farmers instead of rebels. If they wake to rebels, they'll purge everyone. It's protection, by his lights, and it's murder.

    "Now, I've buried more folks than you've ever met, and I loved every one of them. Don't you tell me about mercy."

    Chairwoman Lucienne Adair

    The founder of the Consortium and the first Sleeper to wake. Brilliant, charming and monstrous in her sense of what she's owed, she sees the frontier folk as staff whose contract has expired. Her plan is to "consolidate" the islands, sinking the ones she doesn't need and sending their people down to the surface as labor to build her new cities.

    The thing she won't say: of forty thousand Sleepers, three hundred survived. The vaults failed a century ago. Her nation is a ghost, but whoever holds the vault holds every machine in the sky.

    Plot

    Act I: Heritage (levels 1–15)

    1. The tutorial, the voice on the range, the beacons, the Deputies at the door.
    2. Fairhaven. Ward goes to the Marshal, because he's the law. He's kind. He pours coffee, listens, promises to look into it. That night his Deputies come to collect Ward. A chase through town, the Faithful shouting "demon," and a Hangman in the street.
    3. The Undercroft. Professor Fitch has known about the hatch for years and never dared open it. He and Ward go down. Under the farm are miles of humming machinery, and Ward knows the way. The Caretaker, an old engine mech gone wrong, guards the core.
    4. The record. At a terminal, Ward's own file: W-7. Assignment: Heritage oversight. Status: decommissioned by Warden Prime. Ward comes home in pieces. Clem sits beside them on the porch: "You still burn the biscuits. You're still you."

    Act II: The Dawn Protocol (levels 15–42)

    1. Copperline. Ward needs parts and allies. The Scrappers want Ward for parts, until a Deputy purge hits their island and Ward stands with them. Del Hart joins.
    2. The Signal. The Faithful's cathedral is the relay the Marshal uses to send the Charter sermons to every pulpit. Del wants to tell everyone the truth; Fitch fears the panic. Ward broadcasts. Some islands riot, some rejoice, and the Marshal answers with "culls." Reverend Cole defends his saint's tower to the end.
    3. The Foundry. Shut down Deputy production. On the line, Ward wakes Dime, a Deputy who doesn't know what it is either. The Foreman runs the factory floor.
    4. Memory. Ward's past comes back: the Marshal's protégé, the discovery, the shot on the Anchor.
    5. The Anchor. The Courthouse at noon. The square empties. The Marshal walks out to meet Ward in the street: a real showdown, then a long fight when his skin splits and the old frame underneath keeps coming. Dying, he holds out his star, the master key to everything: "I kept them safe four hundred years by keeping them small. Maybe you'll do better."
    6. The Dawn Protocol completes. Lucienne Adair opens her eyes. The other survivors stay in their pods, waiting on her order.

    Act III: Elysium (levels 42–50)

    1. Lucienne's voice comes out of every pulpit, every Reliquary and every Deputy at once: "Thank you for your service. Your employment is concluded." Her Valets seize the Anchor. Island by island, she begins sinking the habitats she calls redundant, sending their people down.
    2. Elysium. Ward breaks into the vault under the Anchor: silent white halls, gardens under a false sun, a cryo hall of forty thousand pods, almost all of them dark. The Concierge politely tries to kill Ward in every room.
    3. The Chairwoman. Lucienne meets Ward in the Executive Frame, a white-and-gold war chassis built to protect the founders.

    Ending

    With the Marshal's star, Ward holds the vault. They can wake the three hundred survivors as citizens with no power over anyone ("You'll farm like everyone else"), or leave them sleeping forever. Then Ward opens the Descent: the old lift shafts to the surface, sealed for four hundred years. The last scene is the folk of Skyreach seeing the green world below for the first time, and Clem at the edge, grinning at Ward: "Race you down."

    The world

    • The Shroud. The islands float above a white sea of cloud with no known bottom. Whatever falls is gone. Folk say "gone to the Shroud" the way others say "passed on."
    • The Lantern Guild dives anyway: brass rigs, lanterns, and lifelines run from gantries on the islands' rims, winched by a partner who keeps the line.     • The Hush. The deeper you go, the quieter it gets. Sound dies. Compasses turn. Time slips, and you hear voices you know. Divers who stay too long come back hushed, or don't come back.
    • The Rising. Lately things come up. Spiders climb out of the islands' undersides. Wrecks drift up, crusted with glowing orange crystal, the Bloom. And people who fell years ago walk up out of the clouds, unchanged in face and wrong in manner, with orange light behind their eyes: the Returned.
    • The secret. At the bottom of the Shroud lies the old world, and in it the Fathom: a mind the size of a sea, made of living Bloom. It isn't evil. It grows toward whatever falls, holds it, and remembers it. The Returned are its memories of the fallen, rebuilt in Bloom. Long ago the Keepers lifted their mountains out of its reach, and the Reliquaries they left on every island are seals: lullabies in stone, keeping the Fathom's attention asleep. Break an island's seal and the Bloom reaches up beneath it, slowly, and pulls it down.

    Protagonist: Marlowe "Low" Fenn

    The arc is grief. It starts as searching for the past and ends as letting it go. The story's hardest question is put to Low directly: if something walks up out of the clouds with your mother's face, voice and memories, and loves you, is it her?

    Antagonist: Father Abelard Quaile

    • Wreckers: human salvage raiders competing with the Guild in the Fallfield.
    • Creatures:
      • Meadow Wolf spiders, driven up through the islands.
      • MossBacks in the dripping undersides.
      • Cliff Huntsmen clinging upside down to the islands' bellies.
      • Ember Tarantulas crusted with glowing Bloom.
      • Sky Jumpers that come out of the fog.
      • The deep fauna: Lures (anglerfish-like fliers that mimic a diver's lantern), Fathom Eels, Glass Mantas, and Hushmoths, swarms that swallow sound.
    • Bosses: Mother Lure under Lanternhold, Dagny Sorrel in Lowtown, the Choir (a knot of singing Returned) at Saint Ebba's, and Quaile in the Throat.

    Plot

    Act I: The Rising (levels 1–15)

    1. The tutorial, and Imogen on the gantry.
    2. The first night. The Returned climb Skyreach's underside and come over the plateau's edge. Low defends the farm. The Returned part around Imogen as if she weren't there.
    3. Lanternhold. Guildmistress Brask wants Imogen locked up and studied. Low hides her on the farm instead. The Guild's reports are grim: islands are dropping, with Bloom roots on their undersides, and the Sounding is smashing Reliquaries.
    4. The Undersides. Chase Sounding Cutters beneath Lanternhold through the roots and the upside-down Keeper halls. Mother Lure has been luring divers off their lines for years.
    5. Brightwater falls. A neighboring island's seal is broken. Evacuate it while orange roots drag it down: a timed level on a tilting island.
    6. Imogen is gone in the morning. Her note: "I have to show them the way home. Come find me, Low. Come down."

    Act II: The Descent (levels 15–35)

    1. The Fallfield. Low's first truly deep dive, with Tub on the line. Lowtown, the town that fell eighty years ago, lies on the cloudcrust with its lamps lit. The Wreck-Queen Dagny Sorrel claims it. In Lowtown's inn, Imogen's logbook lies on a table, as if someone set it down on the way up. Its last pages are written in a steadier hand than any frightened diver's.
    2. The Hushwood. Bloom trees, total silence, Hushmoths. Sound fails and the HUD stutters. In a Sounding camp, Sister Vey defects.
    3. Saint Ebba's. Quaile preaches to the faithful and the Returned, with Imogen at his side. The Choir rises to stop Low. In Quaile's study, Low learns the truth: the Returned are the Fathom's memories in Bloom. Imogen is a copy, with every memory up to the moment she fell. Quaile shrugs. "What is a person but what they remember?"
    4. Imogen is torn between the thing that made her and the child she remembers raising, and gets Low out.
    5. The Great Sounding. Quaile breaks the great seal beneath Lanternhold. Bloom begins climbing toward the whole Rim.

    Act III: The Floor (levels 35–50)

    1. The Floor. Low goes all the way down, past every record. Down there is the old world, dark and enormous, lit by pools of Bloom, with the Keepers' cities in ruins. Their carvings explain everything: the Fathom isn't hungry. It's lonely. It reaches for whatever falls because it has never been able to keep anything, and the Keepers sang it to sleep and lifted their homes away.
    2. The Throat. Down into the Fathom itself, through rooms built from three centuries of memories. Quaile has given himself to the Bloom and become the Speaker, crowned in crystal on a throne of fallen things. The final fight.

    Ending

    Quaile is gone, but the Fathom is fully awake and reaching. No new seal will hold it. Someone has to go in and talk to it: teach it what letting go means. Imogen takes Low's hand. "I already came back once. That's more than most people get." She walks into the light.

    The Bloom recedes. The islands steady. Tub hauls Low up the longest line the Guild has ever run. Epilogue: Lanternhold at dusk, Low on the gantry. Far below, the clouds glow a faint, slow orange, like breathing.

    • Who makes the guns.     Endgame (levels 50–70)

    The strongest endgame of the eight:

    • Dives. The Shroud never ends. A dive is a descent assembled from layer kits (Fallfield, Hushwood, Floor), and depth is the difficulty. Your lantern oil is the clock, and the Hush builds as modifiers the deeper you go. You choose when to turn back. Deeper loot is better, but you only keep it if you make it back up the line. Die below, and your haul waits at the end of your line, to be recovered once.
    • Rising Tides. The Returned climb an island; hold it until dawn.
    • Lost Divers. Rescue named divers, each a short story.
    • The Depth Board. The Guild's record board: personal bests and records.

    Production notes

    • Reuses: the island art kit, heavily. Lowtown and the Fallfield are the tutorial's own houses, barns and fences, tilted, broken and dusted with Bloom. The waterfall becomes the Spill, the divers' guide line. Lantern posts become dive anchors.
    • New: an underside rock kit (stalactites, hanging roots); cloud floors made of cheap height fog and painted layers (volumetric clouds cost 9.5 ms on Medium, so they're out); a Bloom crystal kit as an emissive surface (Nanite-safe); the Hush (audio and a post effect, measured); and for the endgame, a dive builder that stitches room kits together, the biggest tech item.
    • Risk: medium. Horror needs restraint, and dark, foggy scenes still have to hold 8.3 ms at every viewpoint.

    Why pick it, and what to watch

    Pick it for the strongest endgame loop here (dives scale forever and the fiction explains every rule), a mystery deep enough for a long game, a personal emotional core, and the heaviest reuse of the island kit. Watch out: horror turns some players away (keep the top bright and the dread slow), fog and darkness are easy to make expensive, and Made in Abyss is the obvious comparison. Going down is the shared idea, but a mother who comes back up isn't in that story.

    Cold open

    A woman's voice, unhurried, the way you'd tell a child a story at bedtime: "Once, all the birds held a contest. Whoever flew highest would be king of the birds..." Over it: seventy-seven years ago, a girl in white robes climbs the Stair at the head of a procession of priests and banners, carrying a small burning stone. The Lamp above blazes. The girl walks into the light. The procession sings. And then, so slightly you'd miss it, the Lamp dims.

    Today: a long golden afternoon on Skyreach. Wren Tolliver is asleep in an apple tree, a boot dangling.

    The world

    • The Stair. The islands of the sky rise in a slow, enormous spiral around nothing at all. At the top is the Crown, an island of glass and gold, holding the Lamp: not a star but a fire burning in the sky, the only sun there is.
    • Light is rank. The higher an island sits, the warmer and brighter its days. The High tiers hold the nobility and the Church, in endless summer. The Middle tiers farm and trade. The Low tiers mine in the long twilight. Skyreach sits in the Middle, in the Afternoon, warm and golden.
    • The Kindling. Every seventy-seven years the Lamp burns low, and a Lamplighter climbs the Stair carrying the Wick, a seed of orange crystal, to rekindle it. The Lamplit Church chooses the Lamplighter, crowns them and escorts them up in a procession. Lamplighters never come back. They become one with the Light, the Church says.
    • The Dusk. The last Kindling was seventy-seven years ago, and the Lamp has dimmed every year since. A line of twilight, the Dusk, is climbing the Stair. Below it the islands frost over and the Duskborn come out: animals and people changed by the dark, pale and huge-eyed. The Church says the Dusk is punishment for the sins of the Low.
    • The secret. The Kindling burns the Lamplighter: they become the fuel. The last Lamplighter, Corvina Ashe, a low-born orphan, understood that at the top and refused. She learned to draw the Lamp's fire into herself instead, and became the Radiant: undying, burning, beautiful. She struck a bargain with the Church's Hierarch. She keeps the Lamp lit just enough for the High tiers, and lets the nobility bathe in her light and never age. In return she is worshipped and never replaced. The light is being hoarded at the top. The Low freezes so the High can live forever.
    • The Cinder. The Lamp is cracking under Corvina's draw. A piece of it broke off and fell, looking for someone to carry it back up. The Lamp is trying to save itself.

    Protagonist: Wren Tolliver

    Wren is twenty-two, small, quick, funny and a born climber, the farmhand who gets the top apples nobody else can reach. Their mother Bess raised them on the old fable of the wren and the eagle: the birds' contest to fly highest, won by the smallest bird riding hidden on the eagle's back and flying the last stretch alone.

    When the Cinder chooses Wren, the Church calls them a thief. Wren decides to climb anyway: if the light needs a Lamplighter, it'll get one who asks questions. The arc runs from a nobody to the one who changes the rules, and from refusing the Church's terms to refusing Corvina's too, because the answer was never to burn or to hoard.

    "Small bird. High tree. Long way down. I've done this before."

    Antagonist: Saint Corvina, the Radiant

    Seventy-seven years ago Corvina Ashe was a frightened, brave orphan from Ashe's Rest, a Low island, chosen because the Church always chooses someone nobody will miss. At the top she saw what the Kindling does and refused to die for people who had never once looked down. She is right about the Church. That's what makes her dangerous. Her answer was to become what she hated, a hoarder of light, ruling from the Crown while her own childhood island froze in the dark.

    She is radiant, warm, witty, tired and terrifying, and she sees herself in Wren. She'd rather convert Wren than kill them.

    "They sent you up to burn, little wren. Same as me. Nobody ever asks the candle."

    Hierarch Lucan Mordaunt

    The head of the Lamplit Church: silver-haired, serene and a hundred and fifteen years old in a forty-year-old's body, thanks to Corvina. He chose Corvina and sent her up the Stair, and for decades he has preached the Dusk as the Low's punishment. He fears death more than any sin.

    Aurelian Aquila, "the Eagle"

    The Church's champion: high-born, honorable, trained since birth to escort the next Lamplighter, and the finest flyer on the Stair on his great glider-wings. His orders are to cut the Cinder out of Wren's hand. He's Wren's rival for two acts and two duels, and he slowly realizes his Church is a lie. In the end he carries Wren up through the Glare, like the eagle in the fable.

    "My oath was to the Light, Hierarch. Not to you."

    Plot

    Act I: The Cinder (levels 1–15)

    1. The tutorial, ending with the Cinder.
    2. The Church comes. By morning an Inquisitor and Aurelian are in the Crossing, demanding "the stolen relic." The village hides Wren. Then the shooting starts. Bess and the smuggler Ezra Moss get Wren off the island, down.
    3. The Gloaming. Frozen islands and refugee camps. Bram attaches himself to Wren. The Cinder's light keeps the Duskborn back, which is the first use of its power. On Ashe's Rest, in a frozen orphanage, Wren finds Corvina's childhood diary: a scared, brave girl who wanted to see the sun. The Pale Mother, queen of the moths, comes for the Cinder.
    4. Sister Ilse finds Wren in the camps with the Lamplighter Rolls. None ever came back, and every one was low-born.
    5. The first duel. On the way back up, Aurelian is waiting on Skyreach's lookout. He wins easily. Then the Cinder flares in Wren's hand, refusing him, and he hesitates and lets them go.

    Act II: The Stair (levels 15–38)

    1. The Noon Steppes. Under a sun too harsh to farm by, Kasimir Vell sells water by the cup and has taken the Church's money to catch Wren. Freeing his wells frees the Steppes.
    2. The Windward Spires. The Order of the Kite teaches Wren to glide on the updrafts between the spires. On a rope bridge in the wind, Aurelian's second duel ends with Aurelian falling and Wren catching him. Abbess Sen tells them both the truth about the Kindling.
    3. The Gilded Terraces. Eternal golden light and nobles who never age. Wren infiltrates Margrave Leontine's masquerade and finds where the stolen light goes. In Halo, Hierarch Mordaunt confesses, almost proudly, to the bargain with the Radiant, and fights in his blazing regalia. Aurelian turns his wings on his own Church.
    4. The Dusk surges. Corvina speaks to Wren through the Cinder for the first time: "Come up, little wren. Let me show you what they did to us." The Dusk lurches upward. When Wren comes home, Skyreach is dark, with frost on the windmill and lamps lit at noon.

    Act III: Kindling (levels 38–50)

    1. The Glare. Heat that blisters, mirages and mirrors. The Prism turns Wren's own light against them. No glider can climb the last updrafts alone, so Aurelian carries Wren on his wings, just like the fable. At the very top his wings burn, and Wren flies the last stretch alone.
    2. The Crown. Glass and gold, the caged Lamp, and Corvina: the most beautiful thing Wren has ever seen, and the saddest. She offers Wren what she has, life forever, and when Wren refuses, she fights like the sun.

    Ending

    Wren refuses both answers: burning in the Lamp like every Lamplighter before, or hoarding it like Corvina. With the Cinder they break the cage and scatter the Lamp's fire across the Stair, into the Reliquary on every island. Each island's stone wakes as its own small sun: the Thousand Lamps. No more Lamplighters, and no more light by rank.

    Corvina's stolen fire leaves her, and seventy-seven years arrive all at once. "It's so cold," she says, almost smiling. "I'd forgotten." Wren sits with her until the end.

    Epilogue: Skyreach in the afternoon again. Above the plateau, the island's Reliquary glows like a small sun. Bess is telling Bram the fable of the wren and the eagle, and changing the ending.

    Production notes

    • Lighting is the variety: each tier is one sun angle and color, one sky, one fog. That's cheap to build and cheap to run, and every tier looks distinct. The Gloaming and the darkened Skyreach are the existing meadow kit with frost and twilight. There's no day-night cycle in play: each tier keeps its own fixed light, and the story, not the sky, tells the days passing.
    • New: gliding on updrafts; mirror-shield enemies; a light-radius mechanic (the Cinder keeps the Duskborn back); the procedural climb for the endgame.
    • Risk: medium. The mythic voice needs strong writing, and the "sun is a lamp" myth has to be committed to fully.

    Why pick it, and what to watch

    Pick it for the clearest progression of the eight (higher is harder and brighter), visual variety for the price of lighting rigs, a rival who becomes a friend, and a mythic ending where the Reliquary turns out to be the whole point. Watch out: a fable can feel soft for a shooter, so make the Steppes and the Terraces combat-heavy. The class allegory should be told through people (Corvina, Mordaunt, Bram), not speeches.

    Cold open

    The barn on Skyreach, at night. A tiny old woman in welding goggles tightens one last bolt on something with too many barrels. "Mr. Pockets, take notes. Prototype forty-two. If this works, I'll be famous. If it doesn't, I'll be famous and dead." The training dummy in the corner says nothing, as always. KA-BOOM. The roof lifts off and settles back, mostly. A chicken lands on the weathervane.

    Next morning: a funeral in the orchard. Sunny Pickett in a borrowed suit. The coffin is closed. "Obviously," murmurs the lawyer.

    The world

    • The sky runs on guns. Every island has a gunsmith, and the big ones are dynasties: Bellweather Arms (Hettie's: the finest), Vandersloot Firearms & Fireworks (cheap and everywhere), Regulator & Daughters (precision), Kestrel Aeronautic (bullpups for sky crews), Scrapjack Salvage Co. (welded from junk), Thunderdrum Ordnance (loud) and Zephyr Atelier (art you can shoot).
    • The Bellweather Standard. Seventy years ago Hettie invented interchangeable gun parts: any barrel on any receiver, any stock on any gun. Every maker in the sky uses her standard, which is why every gun is built from parts. In a sense, every gun in the sky is a Bellweather.
    • Hettie herself: inventor, war hero, prankster, five times married ("four husbands and one very confused rival"), and the only gunsmith who ever made a gun that fires preserves. She lived and worked on Skyreach. The barn is her workshop, the windmill drives the lathe in the shed beneath it, the target meadow is her test range, and the dummies are her prototypes.
    • The Masterworks. Twelve legendary guns built across her life, each one hidden inside a trial she built (puzzles, traps, a guardian), each themed on a chapter of her life. Rumor says there's a thirteenth, the Last Word: a gun to end all guns.
    • The secret. Hettie isn't dead. She faked it to draw out the one man who'd found out the Last Word is real.

    Protagonist: Sunny Pickett

    Sunny has been Hettie's farmhand for six years: fed the chickens, mended the fences, caught falling prototypes, endured the jokes. Cheerful, unflappable, wildly underestimated, can't cook. Sunny is also, without knowing it, one of the best shots in the sky, because every chore Hettie ever gave them somehow involved a gun.

    The will names Sunny as a contender alongside the family, and the family is not pleased. The arc runs from "just the help" to Hettie's true heir, and from a people-pleaser who says yes to everyone to someone who will say no to a tycoon's face, and to Hettie's.

    "I'm not here for the company. I'm here because she still owes me six years of chicken feed."

    Antagonist: Mortimer Vandersloot

    The Gun King of Concord: flamboyant, enormous, mustached, his face stenciled on every crate he ships. Hettie's business partner sixty years ago and her third husband for eleven months. He founded Vandersloot Firearms & Fireworks on one idea: a gun in every pot. Make them cheap, make them fast, make them break so people buy more. He despises craftsmanship as a luxury for people who can't sell. Vandersloot guns are the grey commons you find in every crate in the game.

    He wants the Masterworks to copy them by the million. More than that, he wants the Last Word, the gun Hettie built fifty years ago, whose single shot jams every gun in range for good. Fired on every rival factory, it would leave exactly one gunmaker in the sky. He's a comic villain with a real, very modern menace: a monopoly.

    "Quality, my dear, is a luxury for people who can't sell."

    Plot

    Act I: The Will (levels 1–15)

    1. The tutorial, ending with the airships arriving.
    2. The reading of the will. The barn. Quill reads. The cousins sneer. Vandersloot shows up uninvited with a marching band. Then the will names Sunny, and the room explodes. Hettie's phonograph recording plays: "If you're hearing this, I'm dead, or hiding from the Auditor. Either way..." She lays out the hunt, and the first clue sends everyone running for the windmill. First Love is under it, and the brawl follows everyone out of the barn and up the hill.
    3. The County Fair. The trial is a carnival: trick shooting galleries, a hall of mirrors, a Ferris wheel. Big Mama Calibre and her boys want the prize. Blue Ribbon.
    4. Honeymoon Falls. The honeymoon suite holds two Masterworks and a lot of feelings. Percival and Prudence attack in a two-seat walking contraption that mostly works.
    5. Vandersloot makes Sunny an offer over dinner: "Name your price." Sunny thinks about it. "How much for the chickens?" He doesn't get it.

    Act II: The Hunt (levels 15–38)

    1. The Front. Colonel Gus, ex-husband number two, has guarded Hettie's war trial for forty years out of love. Hettie's recordings of the war years are the first time Sunny hears her sad. Then Ol' Bessie, the walking artillery piece she built for the war, wakes up.
    2. Patent Row. The Auditor catches up. The divorce-court trial is a courtroom shooting gallery (cardboard lawyers, real bullets) for Alimony. Doc Spark's clockwork horses get loose. Then the Auditor fights Sunny himself, and he is terrifying. Beaten, he finds something better to do: Sunny shows him Vandersloot's hidden ledgers in the patent office. "Mr. Vandersloot," he says to no one, "we need to discuss your deductions."
    3. The Holy Recoil. The cult has Rainmaker on its altar as a holy relic. Brother Kickback fights in recoil-powered armor: "The Lord giveth recoil, and the Lord taketh away."
    4. Something's off. Hettie's recordings keep mentioning things that happened after she died: the Auditor's ugly tie, the cousins' contraption. Mr. Pockets gets cagey.
    5. The raid. Vandersloot hits Skyreach with everything. The workshop burns, the Masterworks are stolen, and Mr. Pockets is blown apart holding the door. "Tell Hettie... her stitching... was terrible."

    Act III: The Last Word (levels 38–50)

    1. Hettie's Folly. A clue inside Mr. Pockets' remains points to Hettie's first workshop, a floating junkyard of failed prototypes that still move. At its heart, in a chair, in goggles: Hettie Bellweather, alive. "Took you long enough." She faked her death to draw Vandersloot into the open, because he'd learned the Last Word was real. She built it in a fit of guilt fifty years ago and has never been able to decide whether the world deserved it, so she needed an heir who'd decide better. She watched the whole hunt through Mr. Pockets' glass eye. Sunny is furious: "You owe me six years of chicken feed and a funeral." Two more Masterworks wait in the Folly's junk.
    2. Vandersloot City. Take the Masterworks back from the factory island, with the Auditor and a turncoat Prudence alongside. The fireworks plant goes up spectacularly. Colonel Catherine Wheel commands the Fireworks Division, and the Rival is in Vandersloot's trophy vault.
    3. The Expo. Read together, the engravings on the stolen Masterworks give the Last Word's hiding place, and Vandersloot read them first. He unveils it at the Grand Gun Exposition, to an audience of every gunmaker in the sky. The final battle runs through halls of every gun ever made. Encore was in a display case the whole time. Vandersloot climbs into the Mk. Infinity, a walking suit built from a thousand of his own cheap guns. Hettie fights beside Sunny in a prototype of her own. It explodes, as her prototypes do.

    Ending

    Sunny ends up holding the Last Word. Fire it at Vandersloot's factories and his empire of cheap guns jams forever. Lock it away, as Hettie did. Or fire it straight up, and every gun in the hall jams, Hettie's and Sunny's included (Rosa has a crate of spares). Hettie laughs until she cries: "Well. That's one way to retire."

    Vandersloot is arrested by the Auditor for tax fraud, which hurts him more than any bullet. Hettie names Sunny the head of Bellweather Arms. Sunny's first act: rebuild the barn and give Mr. Pockets a new body, with better stitching.

    Loot in this world

    • Who makes the guns. Every maker in the world section, all on Hettie's standard, which is the parts system in the fiction. Vandersloot is the grey common in every crate; Bellweather parts are the prize.
